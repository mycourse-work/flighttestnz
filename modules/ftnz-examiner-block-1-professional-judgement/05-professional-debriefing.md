# Professional Debriefing

A difficult assessment decision is not complete until it can be communicated clearly and professionally.

The debrief should explain the outcome using evidence and standards without turning into criticism, argument or remedial instruction.

## A defensible debrief

- State the outcome clearly.
- Identify the evidence that mattered most.
- Link that evidence to the applicable standard or competency.
- Acknowledge significant positive performance where appropriate.
- Avoid vague labels such as “poor attitude” or “bad judgement” without observable evidence.
- Do not debate reputation, operational need or what another examiner might have done.
- Preserve candidate dignity even where the outcome is unsuccessful.

## Debriefing language

| Less effective | More professional |
| --- | --- |
| “You just showed poor judgement.” | “The assessment concern is that you continued the approach after the agreed stabilisation criteria were no longer met.” |
| “You seemed overloaded.” | “During the arrival you missed the radio call, omitted the checklist and required repeated heading correction. Those observations support the workload-management concern.” |
| “I didn’t like your decision.” | “The decision did not demonstrate the required standard because the available threat information was not incorporated into the plan.” |

## Difficult reactions

Candidates may be disappointed, defensive or upset. Emotional reaction does not alter the standard. Remain calm, clear and respectful. Avoid becoming drawn into personal argument and return to the evidence and applicable assessment criteria.

> **Tip:** Acknowledge without conceding: “I understand that you’re disappointed. Let me take you through the evidence that led to the assessment outcome.”

Professional acknowledgement does not require changing the decision. It preserves respect while returning the conversation to evidence.

## Apply it

Compare these two approaches:

- “I don’t think you showed enough situational awareness today.”
- “During the arrival you did not identify the conflicting traffic despite two opportunities, and the scan was not re-established until after the traffic alert.”

The second is more defensible because it describes observable behaviour and provides a clearer evidence trail for the assessment judgement.
